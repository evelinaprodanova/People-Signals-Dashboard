People Signals

A small tool that pairs a short pulse survey with a short manager survey every quarter, reads them next to pay data, and tells a leader where to look before the exit survey does.

  • Runs in your browser
  • Your data never leaves it
  • Team level only, never individuals
Try it with the demo dataSee how it works

01 · Why it exists

The problem

Exit surveys arrive late

By the time someone fills one in, the decision is made. The useful conversation was months earlier.

The data exists, in pieces

Pulse scores sit in one tool, manager feedback in another, ratings and exits somewhere else. Nobody reads them side by side until something breaks.

Leaders need a page, not a dashboard

A leader has ten minutes. They need two or three places to look and a first question to ask, not forty charts.

02 · The idea in four steps

How it works

1In

Six small files

Teams (headcount, pay, ratings and HRIS data), quarterly survey scores and exits, exits by tenure, pulse items, group data for the equity view, and comments.

2Check

Twelve signals, one status

Pulse and manager moves, falling response, pay and eNPS, new manager, stalled promotions, heavy load, wide span, rating gap, exit clusters and turnover. Each team ends on one rung: Stable, Watch, Slipping or Act.

3Guard

Privacy first

Team level only. Small groups hidden. No individual scores, no individual predictions.

4Out

One page

The top three places to look, a first question for each, and a note on what the data cannot tell you.

03 · Try it

The dashboard

Each quarter, people answer two short surveys: a pulse on how the team is doing and a few questions on manager support. They are read as a pair, and each team gets one status: Stable, Watch, Slipping or Act. Click a team to see its detail. Open the settings to change what counts as a flag.

Load your own data (it stays in this browser)

Team-level files only: one row per team, never per person. Download a template, fill it in and choose the files, or use Paste. Only the first two are required, and each other file switches on another view. Nothing is uploaded.

04 · The method

Why two short surveys, asked together

They answer different questions

The pulse says how people feel. The manager survey says how well they are supported. Which one falls, or both, points to a different conversation.

Same moment, same people

Asking both every quarter puts them on one timeline, with no six-month gap to explain away.

Short enough to answer

A handful of questions each. Long surveys cut response, and everything else gets weaker.

The pair is read three ways: where a team sits against the company, how it moved since last quarter, and whether the manager score tends to move first across all teams, which decides how much weight an early manager signal deserves.

05 · Before the scores move

Early signals

The pulse, response and exits show what has already happened. Three things often come earlier, and they sit in systems a company already runs. Each is measured for the team, never a person.

Manager changes

Share of the team whose manager changed in six months, and how long the current manager has been in role. Read next to the manager score, since a new manager can help or hurt.

Stalled promotions

Share of top-rated people with no promotion or pay change in 18 months or more.

Heavy quarters

An index from open roles, overtime where tracked, leave not taken and the pace question in the pulse. Exits are left out, since they have their own flag.

Each group is counted once, so one event such as a reorg is not counted as three warnings. The signals never raise a separate alarm: every team gets one status, and early signals decide it only when nothing else has fired.

Rung 1

Stable

Nothing has fired, or only one early signal.

Rung 2

Watch

Two or more early signals and no score has moved. The earliest moment to talk to the manager.

Rung 3

Slipping

One score has moved, response is falling, or pay or turnover is flagged.

Rung 4

Act

Pulse and manager score both down, pulse down two quarters in a row, or exits bunched at one tenure point.

Watch is a bet, and the page treats it as one. The backtest card shows how it would be tested.

06 · Context for the scores

Going deeper than the score

A score on its own can mislead. These three views add the context: who stayed silent, whether pay explains the mood, and what sits beyond the pulse.

Counting the people who did not answer

A score only describes the people who answered. If half the team is silent, it says little about them. The dashboard handles this in four ways.

Response sits next to every score

A favorability figure never appears without the share of the team behind it, for both surveys.

A range, not a point

If every silent person felt negative, the true score could be much lower. If every one felt positive, much higher. The width of that range is the honest uncertainty.

An assumption you can change

The slider re-weights each score as if non-responders were a set number of points less favorable. A flag that only appears at a high assumption is weaker.

Silence is a signal

A response rate that falls quarter after quarter gets its own flag. People often stop answering before they leave.

Adding pay

If Total Rewards can share team-level pay, the useful number is the compa-ratio: average pay divided by the market midpoint for the role. A team at 0.90 is paid about 10% under market. Read next to eNPS, it answers a question leaders often guess at: is pay why this team feels the way it does?

Aggregates only

Average compa-ratio and the share of the team under 0.90. Never an individual's pay, and hidden for small groups.

Exceptions beat averages

The dashboard shows teams far from the pattern: low eNPS with fair pay points away from pay, and fair eNPS with low pay points to what is holding people.

A question for Total Rewards

A pay flag opens a conversation with the team that owns pay, who know the level, location and tenure mix behind the number.

Pay can rule itself out

When a team is paid at or above market (from 1.05, few under 0.90) and eNPS is still low or falling, the dashboard points to the manager score, response, exit timing and what people write.

Beyond the pulse

Tools built for this work usually cover four more things. I added each at a level I could do honestly: team level, with the limits stated.

An equity lens

Pay, promotion, exit, pulse and response gaps between groups, small groups hidden. It is for the people who own pay and fairness, and never changes a team's status. A gap is a prompt for a proper analysis.

What goes with staying

Six pulse items against a question about plans to stay, with a range on each. It shows where a conversation could start, not what causes people to stay.

What exits cost

An estimated cost of exits, with the people the team wanted to keep shown apart. The cost rests on an assumption the page states and lets you change.

Manager spans

Widest and average direct reports per team. A manager with sixteen reports is a different problem from a team with a weak manager score, and the survey cannot tell them apart.

07 · What I decided and why

Choices I made on purpose

Teams, not people.

A tool that says which person will leave is unfair, risky under GDPR, and changes how people behave. A team signal starts a conversation. A personal score ends one.

Few flags, ranked.

A leader should leave the page with three questions, not a to-do list of fifteen.

Manager scores are for support.

The manager survey shows where a manager may need help. I would not use it as a verdict, or show it below a minimum number of reports.

Every flag comes with a question.

A number alone invites guessing. The brief says what to ask, and who to ask.

The tool says what it cannot see.

A high rating gap can mean a lenient manager or a strong team. The brief says so.

Say "too few to call".

A small team's number is shown with its range. A manager score with too few answers is hidden, not shown with a warning.

Log what was done.

A flag without an owner and a date is only a worry. The follow-through card shows which flags have an action.

Exits are not all equal.

Losing someone the team wanted to keep counts more, twice by default, and you can change it. Agree what "regretted" means first, or it becomes a judgment about the person who left.

Humans decide.

Nothing here recommends a decision about a person. It points to where a conversation is worth having.

08 · Next steps

Before you rely on it

What I would do first, before treating the page as more than a way to look at the data.

  1. Test each signal against past exits: compare the following six months for teams with and without it, include the uncertainty on small teams, hold some quarters aside, and drop what does not hold up.
  2. Check the lead-lag result on real quarters before giving an early manager signal any weight.
  3. Keep only the early signals that add something once the others are accounted for.
  4. Agree with Total Rewards how compa-ratio is calculated, and compare only within the same level and location band.
  5. Keep manager changes, promotion timing and open roles as a snapshot every quarter, so they can be tested over time. Compare who answers with who does not.
  6. Set thresholds from your own history first, then from the backtest, and keep absolute floors beside them.
  7. Run the equity view with the people who own pay, with legal agreement first, comparing people at the same level and role.
  8. Use confidential person-level answers to find which items go with staying, and take the cost of an exit from Finance.
  9. Take spans from HRIS reporting lines and read them with the manager score and the load index.
  10. Agree what counts as a regretted exit, and mark reorgs and layoffs on the timeline so they are not read as team health.
  11. Move the follow-through log into shared storage with access rules and a record of changes.
  12. Agree purpose, access and retention with legal and employee representatives before the first run.
  13. Start with one leader and one quarter, then ask whether the page changed what they did.
09 · Read this before you trust anything here

Limits and blind spots

The ten that matter most. The big three: it is untested on real data, small teams are noisy, and even team data can identify people.

Untested on real data Evidence

The signals and thresholds are reasoned, not validated. On the demo data they work because it was built to show them. Whether they predict anything real is untested. Use the page to look at team data in a structured way, not as a prediction.

Small numbers are noisy Statistics

On a team of eight, one exit is 12.5% turnover. Hiding small teams protects privacy but hides places where trouble can sit. The page shows ranges, calls turnover high only when the whole range is above the reference, and tags a pulse move smaller than the margin of error.

Even team data can identify people Privacy and law

Filter by team, then tenure, then location, and a group of two appears. In the EU this needs a stated purpose, limited access, retention rules and sometimes a works council. Employees should know what is measured and why. Counts under 3 show as <3. A real build must also block combinations of cuts.

Manager scores on few reports Privacy and fairness

A manager with four reports can be identified from the score, and one unhappy person moves it a lot. The page hides a manager score below a minimum number of answers and drops it from every flag. Use scores to offer support, not to judge.

Equity data is sensitive Privacy and law

These cuts identify people fastest. They need a stated purpose, a data protection assessment, tight access and, in some countries, works council involvement. They stay with the people responsible for fairness, not line leaders.

Non-response is handled, not solved Bias

Response rates, ranges and the slider make the gap visible. They cannot tell me who stayed silent or why. A real version would compare who answered with who did not, by tenure, location and level.

Compa-ratio is not the same as fair pay Pay data

A low ratio can come from a recent promotion round or poor market data for a niche role. A high one can hide a gap inside the team. Level, location and tenure all move it. Use it to decide where to look first, as a question for Total Rewards, not as a pay equity review.

A warning can become a label Behaviour

A team marked early warning can be treated as a problem before anything is wrong, and a manager can be judged by it. The flag is a prompt to ask.

"Regretted" is a judgment Definition

It depends on who decides, and when, and can reflect bias in who managers want to keep. Counts inside one tenure band are very small, so they show as <3 below three. The weight of 2 is my judgment. Agree the definition before the first count.

It does not show whether the action worked Scope

The follow-through card logs an owner, action and date for each flag and compares the team's status then and now. In the demo it lives in your browser. A real version needs shared storage and a record of changes. It still cannot say the action caused the change.

People Signals is open source (MIT license). It is an analysis workbook, not a validated predictor, and it has not been tested on real company data. It runs in your browser and makes no network requests. The demo data is invented. Reference values in the settings are placeholders.
