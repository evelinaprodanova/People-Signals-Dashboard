People Signals

A small tool that pairs a short pulse survey with a short manager survey every quarter, reads them next to pay data, and tells a leader where to look before the exit survey does.

The problem

Exit surveys arrive late

By the time someone fills one in, the decision is made. The useful conversation was months earlier.

The data exists, in pieces

Pulse scores sit in one tool, manager feedback in another, ratings and exits somewhere else. Nobody reads them side by side until something breaks.

Leaders need a page, not a dashboard

A leader has ten minutes. They need two or three places to look and a first question to ask, not forty charts.

The dashboard

Every quarter, each person answers two short surveys: a pulse on how the team is doing, and a few questions on how well their manager supports them. The two are read as a pair. Each team gets one status: Stable, Watch, Slipping or Act. Select a team in the table to see its detail. Open the settings to change what counts as a flag, or to see what the data itself says is normal.

Load your own data (it stays in this browser)

Team-level files only: one row per team, never one row per person. Download a template, fill it in, and choose the files. Only the first two are required. Each other file switches on another view. Delimiters (comma, semicolon, tab) and decimal commas are detected. Nothing is uploaded: the page makes no network requests.

Why two short surveys, asked together

They answer different questions

The pulse says how people feel. The manager survey says how well they are supported. A fall in one, the other, or both points to different conversations.

Same moment, same people

Asking both every quarter puts the numbers on one timeline. There is no six-month gap to explain away, and no manager score that is older than the pulse beside it.

Short enough to answer

A handful of questions each. If the surveys are long, response falls, and everything below gets weaker.

The pair is read three ways. Where a team sits against the company on both scores. How it moved since last quarter: both down, manager down first, pulse down alone, or steady. And whether the manager score tends to move first across all teams, which decides how much weight an early manager signal deserves.

Early signals

The pulse, response and exits tell you what has already happened. Three things often come earlier, and all three sit in systems a company already runs. Each is measured for the team, never for a person.

Manager changes

Share of the team whose manager changed in six months, and how long the current manager has been in role. A new manager can help or hurt, so it is read next to the manager score.

Stalled promotions

Share of top-rated people with no promotion or pay change in 18 months or more. It joins the rating data you already have to career movement.

Heavy quarters

An index from open roles, overtime where tracked, leave not taken and one pulse question on whether people can sustain their pace. Exits are not in it, since they have their own flag.

These three are kept apart on purpose. Manager change, career movement and load are different groups, and each is counted once. Exits are not counted inside the load index, because turnover has its own flag. That keeps one event, such as a reorg, from being counted as three warnings.

The signals do not raise a separate alarm. Every team gets one status, and the early signals decide it only when nothing else has fired.

Rung 1

Stable

Nothing has fired, or only one early signal. It is shown as a chip, not a status.

Rung 2

Watch

Two or more early signals, and the pulse, the manager score and response have not moved. The earliest moment to talk to the manager.

Rung 3

Slipping

One score has moved, or response is falling, or pay or turnover is flagged. Something has started to change.

Rung 4

Act

Pulse and manager score are both down, the pulse has fallen two quarters in a row, or exits bunch at one tenure point.

Watch is a bet, and the page treats it as one. With real data it would be tested against past exits before anyone relied on it. The backtest card shows how.

Beyond the pulse

Tools built for this work usually cover four more things. I added each at a level I could do honestly: team-level, with the limits stated.

An equity lens

Pay, promotion, exit, pulse and response gaps between groups, with small groups hidden. It is a separate view for the people who own pay and fairness, and it never changes a team's status. A gap is a prompt for a proper analysis.

What goes with staying

Six pulse items against a question about plans to stay, with the range on each. It points to where a conversation could start. It does not say what causes people to stay.

What exits cost

An estimated cost of exits, with the people the team wanted to keep shown apart. Leaders act on money, and the cost rests on an assumption the page states and lets you change.

Manager spans

Widest and average direct reports per team. A manager with sixteen reports is a different problem from a team with a weak manager score, and the survey cannot tell them apart.

Adding pay

If Total Rewards can share team-level pay data, the useful number is the compa-ratio: average pay divided by the market midpoint for the role. A team at 0.90 is paid about 10% under market on average. Read next to eNPS and the pulse, it answers a question leaders often guess at: is pay the reason this team feels the way it does?

Aggregates only

Average compa-ratio and the share of the team under 0.90. Never an individual's pay, and hidden for small groups.

Exceptions beat averages

The dashboard shows teams far from the pattern. Low eNPS with fair pay points away from pay. Fair eNPS with low pay points to what is holding people.

A question for Total Rewards

A pay flag opens a conversation with the team that owns pay, who know the level, location and tenure mix behind the number.

Pay can rule itself out

When a team is paid at or above market (from 1.05, with few people under 0.90) and its eNPS is still low or falling, the dashboard stops pointing at pay. It points to the manager score, response, exit timing and what people write.

Counting the people who did not answer

A score only describes the people who answered. If half the team is silent, the score says little about them. The dashboard handles this in four ways.

Response sits next to every score

A favorability figure never appears without the share of the team behind it, for both surveys.

A range, not a point

If every silent person felt negative, the true score could be much lower. If every one felt positive, much higher. The width of that range is the honest uncertainty.

An assumption you can change

The slider re-weights each score as if non-responders were a set number of points less favorable. If a flag only appears at a high assumption, treat it as weaker.

Silence is a signal

A response rate that falls quarter after quarter, on either survey, gets its own flag. People often stop answering before they leave.

How it works

In

Ten small files

Headcount and tenure. The pulse (with eNPS) and the manager survey each quarter, with how many people answered. Comments. Compa-ratios from Total Rewards. Rating distributions. Exits with tenure at exit. Manager and promotion history, open roles and leave from the HRIS. Group data for the equity view, pay per person from Finance, and spans of control.

Check

Twelve signals, one status

Pulse falling. Pair movement. Response falling. Pay below market with low eNPS. Low eNPS despite market-level pay. New manager. Stalled promotions. Heavy load. Wide span. Rating gap against the company. Exits bunched at one tenure point. Turnover against a reference. Each team ends on one rung: Stable, Watch, Slipping or Act.

Guard

Privacy first

Team level only. Small groups hidden. No individual scores, no individual predictions.

Out

One page

The top three places to look, a first question for each, and a note on what the data cannot tell you.

Choices I made on purpose

  • Teams, not people. A tool that says which person will leave is unfair, risky under GDPR, and changes how people behave once they know about it. A team signal starts a conversation. A personal score ends one.
  • Few flags, ranked. A leader should leave the page with three questions, not a to-do list of fifteen.
  • Manager scores are for support. The manager survey points to where a manager may need help. I would not use it as a verdict on a person, and I would not show it below a minimum number of reports.
  • Every flag comes with a question. A number alone invites guessing. The brief says what to ask, and who to ask.
  • The tool says what it cannot see. A high rating gap can mean a lenient manager or a strong team. The brief says so.
  • Say "too few to call". A small team's number is shown with its range, and a flag that rests on a few people is labelled as such. A manager score with too few answers is hidden, not shown with a warning.
  • Log what was done. A flag without an owner and a date is only a worry. The follow-through card shows which flags have an action and which do not.
  • Humans decide. Nothing here recommends a decision about a person. It points to where a conversation is worth having.

Limits and blind spots

These are the ways this idea can mislead, and what I would do about each one.

Untested on real data Evidence

The signals and thresholds are reasoned, not validated. On the demo data they work because the data was built to show them. Whether they predict anything real is untested, and the thresholds are guesses until they are checked against a real history of exits. Until then, use the page as a structured way to look at team data, not as a prediction.

Thresholds from the data are relative Statistics

Setting a line from what is normal across teams (usual movement, top quarter) is better than a guess, but it flags the top quarter even when everyone is fine, and it treats a bad year as normal if every team had one. It needs absolute floors set from past exits beside it. The defaults on this page are my judgment, not a published standard.

The backtest is a first look, not a result Evidence

This version reads four quarters, so there are two test windows, and only the signals with a history can be tested. On the demo data the exits are invented. A real backtest needs many more quarters, a range around every rate, and quarters held aside. Without those it can only mislead.

Small numbers are noisy Statistics

On a team of eight, one exit is 12.5% turnover. Hiding small teams protects privacy but also hides places where trouble can sit. The page shows a plausible range around exit counts, calls turnover high only when the whole range is above the reference, and tags a pulse move that is smaller than the survey's margin of error. It cannot make a small team's numbers solid.

Some leading signals are still missing Coverage

Manager changes, promotions and load are in. Still out: internal moves, reorgs and layoffs, how recently pay was reviewed, who is regretted attrition, and whether earlier flags were acted on. Without them this is an early warning, not an early prediction.

Non-response is handled, not solved Bias

Showing response rates, ranges and an adjustment slider makes the gap visible. It cannot tell me who stayed silent or why. The adjustment is an assumption. A real version would also compare who answered with who did not, by tenure, location and level.

Two surveys, same people Method

The same person answers both, so their mood on the day can colour both answers. That inflates the link between manager score and pulse. Answering the manager questions first can also shape the pulse. I would vary the order and treat the link as softer than it looks.

Survey fatigue Behaviour

Two surveys every quarter is more asked of people. If response drops, the whole picture weakens. The surveys need to stay short, and people need to see something change because of their answers.

Manager scores on few reports Privacy and fairness

A manager with four reports can be identified from the score, and one unhappy person moves it a lot. The page hides a team's manager score below a minimum number of answers, which you can set, and drops it from every flag. Scores should be used to offer support, not to judge.

Across-team links rest on few teams Statistics

With a small number of teams and only a few quarters, one team can swing a correlation. Each correlation now carries a 95% range, and it says so when the range includes zero. The range treats team-quarters as independent, which they are not, so it is too narrow. The numbers show a pattern worth a question. They do not show that managers cause the other results. The cause can run the other way too: a struggling team can pull its manager's score down.

Compa-ratio is not the same as fair pay Pay data

A low ratio can come from a recent promotion round, a newer market, or poor market data for a niche role. A high one can hide a gap inside the team. Above 1.05 means paid above the market midpoint on average. It does not mean paid fairly, so I use it to decide where to look first, not to rule pay out. Level, location and tenure all move it. It is a starting question for Total Rewards, and it does not replace a pay equity review.

People feel pay, not ratios Perception

Most people do not know their compa-ratio. What drives eNPS is what they believe about fairness and what they hear from peers and recruiters. The link between actual pay and engagement is often weaker than leaders expect, and this tool will show that if it is true.

Pay data is sensitive and slow Governance

It needs Total Rewards' agreement, strict access and, in the EU, care around pay transparency rules. It also changes at review cycles, not quarterly, so it is a snapshot beside quarterly scores.

Early signals are proxies Measurement

Overtime, open roles and leave not taken stand in for workload. They miss unrecorded effort and can reflect a team's habits, not its load. Overtime is only tracked for some teams here, and the index averages whatever exists. The pace question in the pulse is the check on the proxies.

Early signals still overlap Statistics

A manager change often arrives with a reorg, which arrives with a heavy quarter. I count each group once, keep exits out of the load index and never let signals raise a separate alarm. That reduces double counting but does not remove it. A real test has to show that each signal adds something on its own.

A gap between groups is not proof Equity

Level, role, tenure and location can create a gap with no unfair treatment, and a pooled figure can hide a gap inside a team or invent one from the mix of teams. Small groups give noisy rates. The view uses one attribute and two groups, and leaves out everyone else, which a real tool must not do carelessly. It is a prompt for a proper analysis by the people who own pay.

Equity data is sensitive Privacy and law

These cuts identify people fastest. They need a stated purpose, a data protection assessment, tight access, and in some countries works council involvement. Showing them to line leaders would invite blame. They stay with the people responsible for fairness.

Items that move together Statistics

A strong team scores well on every pulse item, so across teams they all link with plans to stay. The ranking among them is weak evidence, and it cannot show cause. Person-level data and a method that separates overlapping items are needed, with the answers kept confidential.

Cost is an assumption Measurement

A flat share of a year's pay per exit ignores role, seniority, lost knowledge and how long the role stays empty. It shows scale, not a forecast. "To keep" needs a definition agreed in advance, or it becomes a judgment about the person who left.

Span is not quality Interpretation

A wide span can work for experienced people doing similar work, and a narrow one often means a manager doing the work too. Spans are shown as widest and average only, so no single manager's team can be picked out.

Activity data crosses a line Privacy

After-hours messages or calendar load would measure workload more directly. They also turn a team tool into surveillance, and need legal and employee-representative agreement. I left them out on purpose.

A warning can become a label Behaviour

A team marked "early warning" can be treated as a problem before anything is wrong, and a manager can be judged by it. The flag is a prompt to ask, and the brief says so.

Public benchmarks are coarse Context

Industry-level turnover ignores company stage, role mix, country and seniority. A robotics team in Sofia and a sales team in London have different normal rates. The benchmark is a reference point, not a verdict.

A rating gap can be fair Interpretation

A team may rate higher because it performs higher. The flag asks for a look in calibration. It does not say the manager is wrong.

People adjust once measured Behaviour

If managers learn that a rating gap or a manager score draws attention, answers and ratings drift toward the middle. If a pulse score becomes a target, people start managing the score. Sharing the brief widely, or tying it to reviews, would make this worse.

Even team data can identify people Privacy and law

Filter by team, then tenure, then location, and a group of two appears. In the EU this needs a stated purpose, limited access, retention rules and, in some countries, a works council. Employees should know what is measured and why. Counts under 3 in the tenure split show as <3. A real build also has to block combinations of cuts, which this page does not offer.

Text analysis can mislead AI

A model can invent themes, miss sarcasm, or handle other languages poorly. It would read comments for a person to check. It would never be the last step.

It does not show whether the action worked Scope

The follow-through card logs an owner, an action and a date for each flag and shows the team's status when the action was raised next to its status now. In the demo it lives in your browser only, and with your own data it is kept in memory until you reload. A real version needs shared storage, access rules and a record of changes. It still cannot say the action caused the change.

Real systems are harder Engineering

This page does not connect to an HRIS or a survey tool, handle access rights, or deal with messy exports. That work is real and I have not shown it here.

Tools like this already exist Position

Survey and people-analytics platforms already offer much of this. The value of a project like mine is the judgment in what to show, what to hide and what to leave to people, not the features.

Before you rely on it

  • Test each signal against past exits before trusting it, and drop the ones that do not hold up.
  • Check the lead-lag result on real quarters before giving an early manager signal any weight.
  • Test each early signal against past exits: for every past quarter, compare exits in the following six months for teams with and without the signal, include the uncertainty on small teams, and hold some quarters aside so the thresholds are not judged on the data that set them.
  • Keep only the early signals that add something on their own once the others are accounted for.
  • Agree with Total Rewards how compa-ratio is calculated, and compare it only inside the same level and location band.
  • Keep manager changes, promotion timing and open roles as a snapshot every quarter, so they can be tested over time and not only as today's value. Compare who answers with who does not.
  • Set thresholds from your own history first (usual movement, top quarter), then from the backtest, and keep absolute floors beside them.
  • Run the equity view with the people who own pay, with legal agreement first, and compare people at the same level and role before drawing any conclusion.
  • Use person-level survey answers, kept confidential, to find which items go with staying, and take the cost of an exit from Finance, not from a flat share of pay.
  • Take spans from the HRIS reporting lines, and read them with the manager score and the load index.
  • Split exits into regretted and other, and mark reorgs and layoffs on the timeline so they are not read as team health.
  • Move the follow-through log into shared storage with access rules and a record of changes, so a flag without an action is visible to the person who owns the area.
  • Agree the purpose, access and retention with legal and employee representatives before the first run.
  • Start with one leader and one quarter, then ask whether the page changed what they did.
